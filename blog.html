My ₹36.43

Notes on economics, data, research in progress, and everything in between.

The name is the price of my two cents: two US cents from 1926, carried through a century of inflation and converted to rupees at today’s rate.

The story behind the name

01The phrase

“My two cents” is a self-deprecating hedge: here is my view, and it is worth about as much as the smallest coin you could spare. The American phrase is generally taken as a translation of the older British two-penneth1, and the “two cents” was not abstract: from 1919 to 1932, two cents was exactly what it cost to post a letter in the United States2. The widow’s “two mites” in the Gospels may have lent the idea some moral colour, though that story is about generosity, not opinion3.

The earliest American print use found so far is a March 1926 item by Allene Sumner in the Olean Evening Times, headed My “Two cents’ worth”1. By the late 1930s the opinion sense is unmistakable in running text, as in Archie Binns’s 1937 novel The Laurels Are Cut Down: “in a national emergency we can’t have everyone putting in his two cents’ worth”4. So 1926 is the anchor year.

02The arithmetic

Inflation is measured with the US Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U, U.S. city average, all items, not seasonally adjusted, 1982–84 = 100). The index stood at 17.8 in March 19265. Two cents are scaled by how far the index has risen since then, using the latest published month6, and converted at the most recent euro reference rates published by the European Central Bank78.

03Why it changes

A small script runs every morning at 05:53 IST. It pulls the latest CPI-U from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics’ public API6 and the dollar–rupee rate from Frankfurter, a free service built on ECB reference rates7, then renames this page. Prices drift up month by month, the rupee moves day by day, and the name follows. The ECB publishes no rates on weekends or TARGET holidays, so on those days the name rests too8.

04Fine print

“Worth” depends on the yardstick. CPI asks what the same basket of goods costs. Measured against production-worker compensation, two cents from 1926 was worth about $1.43 in 2024; against GDP per head, about $2.079. The anchor year matters too: prices fell through the Depression, and CPI-U was 13.8 in June 19365, so two cents from then, when the opinion sense was settling in, would be worth a bit more today. Early CPI figures are reconstructions, so treat the paise as decoration.
